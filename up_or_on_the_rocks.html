Up or On the Rocks?

A Time Series Analysis of Restaurant Job Recovery Post-COVID-19

Jason Staats

Updated July 2026

Overview

The food services and drinking places sector includes restaurants, bars, food trucks, and similar establishments where food and drinks are prepared and served. This report examines monthly employment trends from January 2016 through June 2026 using U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Employment Statistics data.

The central question is straightforward: did the industry fully recover from the COVID-19 collapse, or is it still on the rocks? The evidence points to a real recovery, but also to a sector whose post-pandemic growth path looks more uneven than its pre-pandemic climb.

Methodology

The analysis follows a structured time-series workflow: full series visualization, seasonality analysis, autocorrelation analysis, STL decomposition, and multi-model forecasting with holdout validation.

Employment values from March 2020 through June 2021 were replaced with linearly interpolated estimates during decomposition and forecasting. This treats the pandemic-period collapse as an external shock rather than a feature of the underlying trend or recurring seasonal pattern.

Validation design. Four models were trained on January 2016 through December 2024 and validated against January through December 2025, one complete seasonal cycle. Model performance was assessed using Mean Absolute Error.

Full Series

Employment rose from about 10.95 million in January 2016 to a pre-pandemic peak around 12.3 million in 2019. During the COVID-19 pandemic, employment fell abruptly by roughly 5.7 million jobs from February to April 2020. The recovery was rapid but uneven, with employment regaining the March 2020 level by April 2022 and broadly returning to its pre-pandemic range by mid-2023. After that recovery window, growth settled into a more gradual pattern.

COVID-19 Interpolation

Leaving the pandemic collapse in the decomposition and forecasting series would allow a single extreme event to distort the estimated trend and seasonality. The interpolated version bridges the pandemic shock while preserving the broader shape of the series.

Seasonality

Employment follows a durable yearly pattern: it builds through spring, peaks from June through August, and falls into January and February. The timing is stable across years, while the size of the seasonal swing varies.

Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation function remains strongly positive across early lags, reflecting trend persistence. Later negative values are consistent with the annual seasonal cycle pushing winter and summer months in opposite directions.

STL Decomposition

STL decomposition separates the interpolated series into observed, trend, seasonal, and residual components. The trend recovers strongly after the pandemic window and begins to level into 2025, while the seasonal component repeats the same summer peak and winter trough.

Forecasting

To evaluate out-of-sample performance, the models forecast January through December 2025 after training on January 2016 through December 2024. SARIMA produced the lowest average error and was selected for the 2026 forecast.

SARIMA was retrained on the full series and used to forecast 2026. The model overestimated employment early in the year, but actual employment caught up by spring and moved above the projected path in May and June. May employment came in at 12.57 million against a 12.44 million forecast, while June reached 12.64 million against a 12.40 million projection.

Key Findings

  • Food services employment grew steadily from approximately 11 million in early 2016 to a pre-pandemic peak near 12.3 million.
  • During the early COVID-19 shock, employment fell by more than 5 million jobs in a matter of weeks.
  • Employment returned to the pre-pandemic range during the 2022-2023 recovery window and reached a new high in June 2026.
  • A consistent seasonal pattern persists, with employment peaking in June through August and bottoming in January and February.
  • SARIMA performed best in validation, but June 2026 exceeded the forecast baseline enough to raise the question of renewed acceleration.
