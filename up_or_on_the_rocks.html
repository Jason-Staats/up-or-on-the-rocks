Up or On the Rocks?

A Time Series Analysis of Restaurant Job Recovery Post-COVID-19

Jason Staats

Updated September 2026; data through August 2026

Overview

The food services and drinking places sector includes restaurants, bars, food trucks, and similar establishments where food and drinks are prepared and served. This report examines monthly payroll employment from January 2016 through August 2026 using U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Employment Statistics data.

The central question is straightforward: did the industry fully recover from the COVID-19 collapse, or is it still on the rocks? Employment remained above its pre-pandemic peak from April through August 2026, while the eight recorded 2026 observations remained above the SARIMA forecast and within both prediction intervals.

Methodology

The analysis follows a structured time-series workflow: full-series visualization, COVID-period interpolation, seasonality analysis, autocorrelation analysis, STL decomposition, and multi-model forecasting with holdout validation.

Employment values from March 2020 through June 2021 were replaced using linear interpolation based on time. The interpolated series is used for subsequent seasonality, autocorrelation, decomposition, and forecasting analyses. It imposes a smooth hypothetical path through the selected period and should not be interpreted as an estimate of actual employment.

Validation design. Four models were trained on the interpolated series from January 2016 through December 2024 and validated against recorded employment from January through December 2025, one complete seasonal cycle. The selected SARIMA model was then fitted through December 2025 to forecast January–December 2026, with 80% and 95% prediction intervals.

Full Series

Before diving in, it’s worth clarifying what “food services and drinking places” actually includes. This category covers restaurants, bars, food trucks, and other establishments where food and drinks are prepared and served to customers, whether that’s full-service dining, fast food, or takeout.

Employment in food services and drinking places grew steadily from about 10.95 million in January 2016 to a peak of roughly 12.32 million in August 2019. Going into 2020, that upward trend appeared likely to continue. Instead, employment fell sharply during the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, declining by about 5.7 million jobs from February to April, nearly 50% of total employment in the industry.

Sharp increases in employment were reported in May and June 2020, but the industry didn’t fully recover to February 2020 levels until June 2022, more than two years later.

Since then, growth has been more gradual, with employment generally trending upward despite the seasonal fluctuations visible in the series. In April, employment rose above the pre-pandemic peak for the first time in 2026. Employment continued to rise through June, reaching about 12.64 million, before easing slightly in July and August. Even with that slight pullback, employment has remained above the pre-pandemic peak for five consecutive months in 2026.

Note: This data comes from the Current Employment Statistics (CES) and reflects payroll positions, not individual workers. If someone holds two restaurant jobs, they are counted twice. At the same time, workers paid off payroll are not included. So this is best interpreted as a measure of formal payroll activity in the industry rather than a count of unique people.

COVID-19 Interpolation

The pandemic created an extended external shock rather than a change driven by the underlying trend or normal seasonality in the data. Leaving those observations in place could allow the disruption to strongly influence estimates of both trend and seasonality.

To limit that influence, employment values from March 2020 through June 2021 were replaced using linear interpolation based on time. Rather than modeling the collapse and recovery directly, this approach imposes a smooth path between the observations immediately before and after the selected period. The interpolated values represent a hypothetical series without the recorded pandemic disruption and should not be interpreted as estimates of actual employment during those months.

The original series is shown alongside the interpolated series above for transparency, and all subsequent analysis is based on the interpolated data.

Seasonality

Employment in food services follows a clear seasonal pattern. It generally builds through the spring, reaches its highest levels during the summer months from June through August, and then tapers off through the fall before reaching its lowest levels in January and February.

The timing of this pattern remains broadly consistent across years. The pandemic period should be interpreted cautiously, however, because the actual employment values from March 2020 through June 2021 were replaced through interpolation. The smooth pattern shown during that period reflects the modeling approach rather than the recorded collapse and recovery.

The year-over-year overlay provides a broader view of employment levels. It shows steady growth from 2016 through 2019, a smooth interpolated transition during the pandemic period, and a gradual return to growth in the years since. Because the monthly averages combine years with different overall employment levels, they provide descriptive evidence of seasonality rather than a pure seasonal estimate.

The STL decomposition provides stronger evidence of the recurring monthly pattern. The timing of the seasonal changes remains broadly consistent, while their magnitude varies somewhat across years. Some summers show larger seasonal employment increases than others, suggesting that the timing of the employment cycle is relatively stable even though the size of the seasonal movement can vary.

Note: Monthly averages for January through August include 2026 observations, while September through December are based on one fewer observation because 2026 data are not yet available for those months.

Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation function shows how strongly the employment series relates to its previous values at different time lags. A high positive correlation means that months with relatively high employment tend to follow other months with relatively high employment, while values near zero indicate little linear relationship at that lag.

The correlation is strongest at the shortest lags, measuring approximately 0.91 at one month and declining over the following several months. This is consistent with a persistent employment series in which adjacent months usually have similar levels. The decline is not completely steady, with correlations rising modestly again around lags 10 through 12, offering additional evidence of the annual seasonal pattern.

After lag 12, the correlations decline toward zero. They move below zero beginning at lag 17, but the correlations from around lag 15 onward fall within the displayed confidence bounds. These small positive and negative values therefore should not be treated as evidence of a meaningful inverse relationship.

Using 24 lags provides a two-year view of the series, but the correlation at lag 24 is close to zero. The strongest feature of the plot is short-term persistence, with the smaller increase around lags 10 through 12 reinforcing the evidence of annual seasonality. Because the ACF is calculated from employment levels that contain a long-term trend, it cannot cleanly separate trend persistence from seasonal dependence. The STL decomposition in the next section separates the series into trend, seasonal, and residual components, providing a clearer view of the recurring seasonal pattern.

STL Decomposition

STL decomposition separates the employment series into three estimated components, trend, seasonal, and residual. Together these components provide a clearer view of the long-term direction of employment, its recurring monthly pattern, and the variation not explained by either component.

The trend component shows steady growth through 2019, followed by a smooth decline during the interpolated pandemic window. Employment then recovers through 2023 before leveling off through much of 2024 and 2025. The trend turns upward again toward the end of the series as employment reaches new highs. Because trend estimates near the beginning and end of a series are more sensitive to the available observations, the most recent movement should be interpreted cautiously.

The seasonal component estimates the recurring monthly pattern after accounting for the longer-term trend. It reinforces the pattern seen earlier, with employment generally increasing through the spring, reaching its highest seasonal levels during the summer, and declining through the fall and winter.

The residual component captures the variation remaining after the estimated trend and seasonal components have been removed. Larger and more sustained deviations are visible around the interpolated pandemic period and the subsequent recovery, while the residuals become noticeably smaller in the later years of the series, particularly from around 2024 onward. This suggests that the estimated trend and seasonal components account for a greater share of the recent variation in employment. The small residuals near the end of the series should still be interpreted cautiously because STL estimates at the boundary are based on fewer surrounding observations.

The observed panel reflects the interpolated series rather than the original recorded data. In particular, the smooth transition from March 2020 through June 2021 results from linear interpolation and should not be interpreted as actual employment during that period.

Forecasting

To evaluate how well each model could predict unseen data, calendar year 2025 was held back as a validation set representing one complete seasonal cycle. Four models were trained on data from January 2016 through December 2024 and then used to forecast January through December 2025. Their predictions were compared with the recorded 2025 values using Mean Absolute Error, which measures the average absolute difference between the forecast and actual employment levels.

The SARIMA specification used here was selected as a reasonable starting point for a monthly seasonal series, using first-order autoregressive and moving-average terms with both regular and seasonal differencing. Several nearby model orders were also tested, but they did not produce a meaningful improvement in validation performance, so the simpler specification was retained.

SARIMA produced the lowest validation error, missing the actual 2025 employment level by approximately 46,000 jobs per month. Against monthly employment of roughly 12 million, that represents an average error of less than one-half of one percent. The Seasonal Naive model performed nearly as well, with an MAE of approximately 49,000 jobs, indicating that the previous year’s monthly pattern provided a strong forecasting baseline. ETS and OLS produced substantially larger errors. The OLS model included a linear trend and monthly seasonal indicators, but its fixed trend and seasonal effects were less flexible than the patterns captured by SARIMA.

Based on the validation results, SARIMA was fitted to the interpolated series through December 2025 and used to forecast calendar year 2026. The model projects employment rising from approximately 11.96 million in January to 12.61 million in June. It then forecasts a modest decline to 12.57 million in July, a slight increase to 12.59 million in August, and an overall decline through December, interrupted by a small increase in October.

Recorded employment from January through August 2026 remained above the SARIMA forecast in every month. The differences ranged from approximately 29,000 jobs in August to 72,000 in April, with August producing the smallest forecast error so far this year. Across the first eight months of 2026, the model’s Mean Absolute Error was approximately 44,800 jobs.

The close alignment through August suggests that SARIMA has continued to capture the broad level and seasonal movement of employment reasonably well. Its MAE through the first eight months of 2026 is slightly lower than the 45,851-job error observed during the 2025 validation period, although the two figures are not yet directly comparable because the validation period covers a complete 12-month seasonal cycle. All recorded 2026 values through August also fall within both the 80% and 95% prediction intervals, indicating that observed employment has remained comfortably within the range of outcomes anticipated by the model. The relatively low forecast error is also consistent with the STL decomposition, where residual variation has been comparatively small in the most recent years. Together, these results suggest that recent employment has followed its underlying trend and seasonal structure fairly closely, which may help explain why the SARIMA model has continued to perform well through August 2026.

The model has consistently underestimated employment so far in 2026, but the differences remain small relative to the industry’s total employment level. The prediction intervals generally widen over the forecast horizon, reflecting greater uncertainty in later months. These results should still be interpreted cautiously because the model was fitted to a series containing interpolated values during the pandemic period, the most recent forecast evaluation covers only part of 2026, and recent CES estimates are subject to revision. As the BLS updates previously published values, the reported 2026 forecast errors may change.

Key Findings

  • Employment grew from approximately 10.95 million in January 2016 to a pre-pandemic peak of 12.32 million in August 2019.
  • Recorded employment declined by approximately 5.7 million payroll jobs between February and April 2020. Employment first exceeded its February 2020 level in June 2022.
  • Employment reached approximately 12.64 million in June 2026 before easing slightly in July and August, both of which round to 12.62 million. It remained above the pre-pandemic peak for five consecutive months, April–August 2026.
  • The sector retains a recurring seasonal cycle, with employment generally highest from June through August and lowest in January and February. Patterns in the interpolated pandemic window reflect the modeling treatment.
  • SARIMA produced the lowest 2025 validation MAE at 45,851 jobs, narrowly outperforming Seasonal Naive.
  • Recorded employment exceeded the forecast in all eight months from January through August 2026. Errors ranged from approximately 29,000 jobs in August to 72,000 in April; eight-month MAE was approximately 44,800 jobs.
  • All eight recorded 2026 values fell within both the 80% and 95% prediction intervals. The partial-year evaluation and interpolated modeling series remain limitations, and BLS revisions may change the reported errors.
