Up or On the Rocks?

A Time Series Analysis of Restaurant Job Recovery Post-COVID-19

Jason Staats

Updated August 2026; data through July 2026

Overview

The food services and drinking places sector includes restaurants, bars, food trucks, and similar establishments where food and drinks are prepared and served. This report examines monthly payroll employment from January 2016 through July 2026 using U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Employment Statistics data.

The central question is straightforward: did the industry fully recover from the COVID-19 collapse, or is it still on the rocks? Employment has now moved above its August 2019 pre-pandemic peak, while the 2026 observations remain closely aligned with the seasonal forecast.

Methodology

The analysis follows a structured time-series workflow: full-series visualization, COVID-period interpolation, seasonality analysis, autocorrelation analysis, STL decomposition, and multi-model forecasting with holdout validation.

Employment values from March 2020 through June 2021 were replaced with linearly interpolated values for decomposition and forecasting. The interpolation imposes a smooth hypothetical path through the pandemic disruption and should not be interpreted as an estimate of actual employment during those months.

Validation design. Four models were trained on January 2016 through December 2024 and validated against January through December 2025, one complete seasonal cycle. The selected SARIMA model was then fitted through December 2025 to produce a true calendar-year 2026 forecast.

Full Series

Employment grew from about 10.95 million in January 2016 to approximately 12.32 million in August 2019. The onset of COVID-19 then eliminated roughly 5.7 million payroll jobs between February and April 2020, nearly half of the sector's employment.

Employment did not fully return to its February 2020 level until June 2022. Growth was more gradual afterward, but 2026 marked another shift. Employment reached 12.66 million in June and remained near that level at 12.62 million in July. Both months exceeded the August 2019 pre-pandemic peak in raw payroll employment.

COVID-19 Interpolation

The pandemic created an extended external shock rather than a movement driven by the underlying trend or normal seasonality. Employment values from March 2020 through June 2021 were replaced through linear interpolation to keep that disruption from dominating estimates of trend and seasonality.

The interpolated values represent a hypothetical series without the recorded pandemic collapse and recovery. The original and interpolated series are shown together for transparency; subsequent decomposition and forecasting use the interpolated series.

Seasonality

Employment follows a clear recurring pattern. It generally builds through spring, reaches its highest levels from June through August, and then declines through fall before reaching its lowest levels in January and February.

The timing remains broadly consistent across years, although the magnitude of the seasonal movement varies. Monthly averages provide descriptive evidence, while the STL seasonal component offers a clearer view of the recurring pattern after accounting for the longer-term trend.

Autocorrelation

Autocorrelation is strongest at the shortest lags, measuring approximately 0.91 at one month and then declining. Correlations rise modestly again around lags 11 and 12, providing some evidence of the annual seasonal pattern.

After lag 12, the correlations move toward zero and cross it near lag 17. Values from roughly lag 15 onward fall within the displayed confidence bounds and should not be interpreted as meaningful inverse relationships. Because the ACF is calculated from employment levels containing a long-term trend, short-lag persistence cannot be cleanly separated from seasonal dependence.

STL Decomposition

STL decomposition separates the interpolated employment series into trend, seasonal, and residual components. The trend grows through 2019, moves smoothly through the interpolated pandemic window, recovers through 2023, and levels off through much of 2024 and 2025 before turning upward again in 2026.

The seasonal component reinforces the recurring summer peak and winter trough. Most residuals remain close to zero, although several periods contain larger deviations. The apparent movement near the end of the trend should be interpreted cautiously because endpoint estimates are more sensitive to the available observations.

Forecasting

Four models forecast January through December 2025 after training on data through December 2024. SARIMA produced the lowest validation error, missing recorded employment by approximately 46,000 jobs per month. Seasonal Naive performed nearly as well, showing that the prior year's monthly pattern remained a strong baseline.

SARIMA was fitted to the interpolated series through December 2025 and used to forecast calendar year 2026. It projects a seasonal rise from about 11.96 million jobs in January to 12.61 million in June, followed by a modest decline through the second half of the year.

Recorded employment remained slightly above the SARIMA forecast in every month from January through July. The gaps ranged from approximately 39,000 jobs in March to 72,000 in April. June reached 12.66 million against a 12.61 million forecast, while July reached 12.62 million against 12.57 million. Mean Absolute Error through July was approximately 49,000 jobs.

Key Findings

  • Food services employment exceeded its August 2019 pre-pandemic peak in both June and July 2026.
  • The pandemic eliminated approximately 5.7 million payroll jobs from February to April 2020, and employment did not recover to its February 2020 level until June 2022.
  • The sector retains a clear seasonal cycle, with employment generally peaking from June through August and reaching its lowest levels in January and February.
  • SARIMA produced the lowest 2025 validation error at 45,851 jobs per month, narrowly outperforming the Seasonal Naive baseline.
  • Recorded 2026 employment stayed slightly above the SARIMA forecast through July, but the average gap of roughly 49,000 jobs remained small relative to total sector employment.
  • The 2026 comparison is a point-forecast assessment; the notebook does not include forecast intervals, and its pandemic interpolation is a counterfactual modeling choice.
